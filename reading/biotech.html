One level deeper

What a priority voucher review buys, and how to rebuild the clock when the lane closes
A PDUFA goal date is a negotiated commitment: for a new molecular entity, FDA aims to act within 10 months of the 60-day filing date on standard review and 6 months on priority, so 12 and 8 months from submission. The Commissioner's voucher was never part of that agreement. It was a pilot run by an executive council that promised a 1 to 2 month review by pulling reviewers together, with no statute or user-fee commitment behind it, which is why it can go dormant without a formal announcement and why FDA can truthfully say the work "is ongoing" while timelines revert to career staff. When you model a vouchered drug, carry two clocks: the pilot clock as upside and the PDUFA priority clock as the base, and weight them by whether the agency has recently acted on anything inside 60 days. For a Schedule I substance like psilocybin, add a second gate: after approval, DEA has up to 90 days to issue an interim final rule rescheduling the product, and many states must then act before a pharmacy can stock it. Approval date and first-sale date can sit a quarter apart for that reason alone. The same discipline applies to MoonLake this month: day 60 tells you only that the BLA is filed, and the day-74 letter tells you which clock you are on. Price the event you are actually getting, and treat any timeline that depends on agency discretion as a distribution, not a date.
