What mattered

Tech
Nvidia board opens independent probe of Huang; stock down 4 to 5% premarket
On November 8 Nvidia's board said a committee of independent directors with outside counsel will investigate CEO Jensen Huang after a New York Times report that he was targeted by a Chinese intelligence operation; Huang emailed staff admitting an affair and the board said it has no reason to believe secrets were compromised and that he stays CEO. Three House committee chairs asked Huang for a closed-door briefing on November 18. Shares fell 4.5% premarket and Nvidia reports fiscal Q3 on November 17.
So what: Read: the fundamental question is not the affair, it is whether a national-security framing gives Congress a new handle on Nvidia's China sales (H20 class exports) three weeks before the print. The probe has no end date, so the overhang sits on the index through CPI and the November 17 report. Winners are the equal-weight S&P and AMD/Broadcom on relative-weight rebalancing; losers are the Nasdaq-100 and Japan semis (Advantest, SoftBank) that trade as Nvidia proxies.

Geopolitics
China PPI 5.7%, fastest since June 2022, while CPI slips to 0.7%
October producer prices rose 5.7% year on year against a 5.3% Reuters poll and 5.0% in September, the third straight acceleration; mining and raw materials (about 29% of the index) contributed roughly 4.0 of the 5.7 points. CPI eased to 0.7% from 0.9% (0.8% expected) and core CPI fell to 0.8% from 1.0%. October trade data lands Tuesday; September exports were up 36.3%.
So what: Read: this is a margin squeeze, not reflation. Oil's 17% drop takes some of that pressure off next month. For US importers of Chinese goods the direction is clear: landed costs rise.

Take: The build-out is bifurcating: cash-rich hyperscalers keep committing (Google $17B Wyoming, $15B Malaysia, AWS 300 MW Washington, Lambda $33.1B Alabama all in the last week) while debt-funded neoclouds lose equity access and Microsoft trimmed its 2026 capex guide because leased capacity slipped. Capex
Microsoft cuts 2026 capex guide to $170B from $175B on third-party capacity delays
CFO Amy Hood lowered calendar-2026 capex to about $170B from $175B, citing delayed GPU-as-a-service capacity from third parties now expected in the second half of the fiscal year. So what: Read: this is the first hyperscaler to say delivered power and shell, not GPUs, is the binding constraint. Developers that can deliver energized capacity on schedule capture the deferred spend; neoclouds that miss delivery dates lose anchor tenants and then financing.

PJM capacity at the $329/MW-day cap into the December 15 auction
PJM capacity prices rose from $28.92/MW-day for 2024-25 to $329.17 for 2026-27 and have been pinned at the federal cap of about $325 for three straight auctions; the market monitor attributed 63% ($9.3B) of the 2025-26 increase to data centers. Opportunity radar

Buy stranded powered sites from stressed neoclouds, sell delivered capacity to hyperscalers who now say delivery is the constraint
Acquire or option permitted, interconnect-queued data center sites (ideally with behind-the-meter gas rights in ERCOT or the inland West) from developers whose equity and high-yield funding closed in the last four weeks, then flip the position to a hyperscaler or its build partner. The buyer who pays is the hyperscaler balance sheet: Google just took over Crusoe's Wyoming site with a $17B commitment, and Microsoft said $5B of 2026 spend slipped because leased capacity was late.
Why now: Three things changed in one week: Democrats swept Congress on a data-center-electricity message, which raises permitting risk in grid-connected PJM and Democratic-trifecta states and makes already-permitted or behind-the-meter sites scarcer; CoreWeave lost half its value with CDS at records, so neocloud developers need to sell assets; and Microsoft named delivered third-party capacity, not chips, as its bottleneck. Forced sellers and a motivated buyer with no financing constraint rarely overlap this cleanly.
Upside: Read, not a fact: entitled land with secured power has been trading at multiples of raw land cost, and a 100 to 300 MW site with gas and water rights is the unit hyperscalers buy whole. The Wyoming transfer shows the exit exists at the gigawatt scale. The prize for a small operator is the spread between a distressed developer's carrying cost and a hyperscaler's cost of delay on $170B of annual capex.
First step: Pull the Texas PUC and ERCOT large-load interconnection queue for projects sponsored by neoclouds or their SPVs (CoreWeave, Iren, Nebius, Applied Digital, Crusoe affiliates), cross-reference against county permit filings in Childress, Ector, and the Panhandle, and identify sites where the sponsor's public debt trades below 80 cents. Then call the land agent of record on two of them.
