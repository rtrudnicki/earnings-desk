Market pricing. About 80% for a 25 basis point hike at the December 8-9 meeting (CME FedWatch 79.6% hike, 20.4% hold, as reported the morning of October 30; above 80% per FXStreet after the October 28 hold), with the ECI print giving no reason to move it.

Guidance. September SEP median fed funds 4.1% end-2026 (one more hike), 4.1% end-2027, 3.9% end-2028, 3.2% longer run; core PCE 3.4% in 2026 and 2.5% in 2027; unemployment 4.1%; GDP 2.3%. What the market is debating

  1. The US-Iran war is the inflation engine: Brent touched $110 and WTI settled $101.41 on Friday, gasoline rose 9.1% in September, and that pushed headline CPI to 3.7% and PPI up 1.3% in one month, the most since 2022; oil opened $3-4 lower Monday on Vance's de-escalation comments.
  2. The Fed has turned hawkish under Warsh with a 25bp hike to 3.75-4.00% in September, and the market debate is October versus December for the next one: hold priced about 85% for October 28 and a December hike about 80%, with core CPI at 2.6% and core PCE at 3.0% deciding it.
  3. Growth and jobs disagree: GDPNow has Q3 at 3.6% and both ISMs are mid-50s, while payrolls added only 29,000 in September with net negative revisions and unemployment ticked to 4.2%; the October 29 advance GDP and November 6 jobs report settle which side the Fed weighs.
  4. The consumer is paying for the war: Conference Board confidence 81.9 is the lowest since 2014, UMich 46.3 sits near its record low, wages at 3.0% trail 3.7% inflation, and September retail sales' 0.8% gain was about 70% gasoline with the control group up only 0.1%.
  5. The long end is repricing a higher-for-longer world: the 10-year at 5.09% and the 30-year at 5.43% after touching 5.19% and 5.52% on Wednesday, the 2s10s curve at +35bp, and the November 4 refunding with a live debate on cutting 20-year issuance.
  6. AI stocks are the equity fault line: the Nasdaq fell 4.5% last week, its worst since June, on an AI-chip rout and financing worries, the S&P 500 lost 1.9% to 7,659 and the VIX closed at 19.99, with the heaviest earnings week of the season ahead.
  7. Housing is frozen by 7% mortgage rates: existing sales under 4 million with 4.8 months of supply, builder confidence at a three-year low of 32 with 38% of builders cutting prices, and starts at 1.275 million with multifamily down 21.7% in a month.
  8. Credit has not blinked: IG spreads at 84bp and high yield at 323bp are flat to tighter in October even with the 10-year above 5%, so the stress is in CCCs at about 1,250bp and in equities, not in the broad credit market.
  9. Is the rate shock reaching the real economy: builder sentiment at 29, the lowest since 2012, with buyer traffic at 18 says yes for housing, while the 3.6% GDPNow says not yet for the whole economy.
  10. Does the oil shock spread into core: Canada's September print says no so far (headline 3.1%, core 1.8% to 1.9%), the opposite of last week's hot US CPI and PPI.
  11. A December hike is still the base case at 75% to 90% priced, but today's weak housing data and softer oil are the first two arguments against it since the Chair spoke on Friday.
  12. Housing recession versus 3.6% GDP: with the NAHB index at 29 (lowest since June 2012) and mortgage rates at 7.5%, today's starts print tests whether residential construction is now subtracting from growth while GDPNow still says 3.6%.
  13. The Fed hike debate moved from October to December: futures price only an 18% chance next week but about three hikes by July 2027 with headline CPI at 3.7% and Brent near $110.
  14. Europe is diverging: euro-area ZEW 18.5 versus 24.2 expected and the France-Germany spread at 148 bp put the ECB's France problem back in front of the euro.
  15. Energy pass-through is spreading beyond the US: UK services inflation rose to 3.7% from 3.4% and headline CPI to 3.5%, while US gasoline stocks drew 2.95M bbl against 0.4M expected.
  16. The slowdown debate lost its last indicator: the LEI went flat in September with its six-month rate at +0.6%, so the 3.6% GDPNow track has no mainstream dissent left.
  17. Labor softness moved from firing to rehiring: initial claims 194k (three-month low) against continuing claims 1,728k (six-week high).
  18. Oil and bonds disagree: Brent above $111 while the 10-year fell 13 bp to 5.02% and October hike odds sank to 8%, so the market reads the energy shock as growth-negative rather than inflationary.
  19. Growth is not the problem: the US composite PMI at 57.6 sits near a five-year high while Europe's slips to 51.9, so Thursday's sub-5% 10-year was an equity and positioning move, not a data move.
  20. Inflation expectations are ratcheting with oil: Michigan's long-run 3.5% and one-year 4.7% are both five-month highs and above the whole 2024 range, while services firms report the fastest price rises since August 2022.
  21. The December hike is priced but not settled: three-quarters odds and a 7.5% two-hike tail with Brent at $114 means the next 10 dollars in oil, not the next print, decides how far the Fed goes.
  22. The growth-versus-prices split inside the December hike debate sharpened today: Dallas Fed production fell to 15.8 from 29.5 while prices paid rose to 55.3, so the case for a hike is now resting on input costs, not on overheating demand.
  23. Activity is not slowing enough to stop the Fed: the Chicago Fed index at +0.07 and GDPNow at 3.5% for the third quarter leave the hold-then-hike path intact two days before the meeting.
  24. The consumer-cracking debate flipped today: confidence at 91.7 vs 81 expected, with September revised up, means the September collapse was a gasoline shock rather than a labor-market break.
  25. The capex-boom-versus-flat-manufacturing debate stayed open: core capex +0.5% against Richmond Fed new orders at -8 and backlogs at -21 says the strength is in aircraft and a few large orders, not the factory floor.
  26. Housing reframed from recovery to stabilization: new home sales beat at 676k, but prices fell in real terms for a 15th month and supply sits at 8.4 months, which keeps shelter on the Fed's disinflation side.
  27. Oil-shock tightening went from debate to action: the Bank of Canada hiked to 2.50% with Brent averaging about $125 in October, the first G7 move on this shock, and the Fed meets with December priced at 76.5%.
  28. The AI capex boom shows up in the trade data: capital goods imports jumped a record 7.5% and pushed the goods deficit to $134.9B, the widest since March 2025, which subtracts from GDP while flagging demand that is anything but weak.
  29. UK fiscal risk stepped back: £15B of headroom was enough for gilts (10-year 5.34%, down 1.4 bp), so the global long end is taking its cue from Washington today, not London.
  30. Growth versus inflation is no longer a trade-off: GDP 3.4% with quarterly core PCE 2.5%, yet monthly core at 0.3% twice keeps the December hike near 85%.
  31. The oil shock is passing through the quarterly deflators (PCE prices 2.1% vs 5.0%) faster than the monthly year-over-year rates (3.6% headline), so which series the Fed weights decides December.
  32. Two central banks with hike dissents in the same week: the Fed's 10-2 and an ECB that debated a hike at 3.8% inflation, with Lagarde leaving February 7, 2027.
  33. Wages are not the inflation problem: compensation 3.4% on the year and wages 3.1% and slowing, against headline PCE at 3.6%, so the December hike debate is an energy debate and oil at $113 to $117 is the whole argument.
  34. The AI-spending worry has spread from the spenders to the suppliers: Intel down 26% to 29% and the Nasdaq down 2.6% while the 10-year fell to 4.93%, the first day in weeks that lower yields came from fear rather than from softer data.
  35. The ECB December hike lost its certainty: euro area GDP beat at 0.4% in the morning, then German inflation missed at 2.8% against 3.0% with core 2.1%, and the euro gave the whole gain back.
