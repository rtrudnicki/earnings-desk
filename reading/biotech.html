One level deeper

Why a PFS hazard ratio of 0.52 was not approvable: overall survival as the benefit-risk test
Summit's CRL is a clean case of FDA treating OS as a required safety and benefit check, even when PFS is the primary endpoint and wins by a wide margin. PFS is measured by scans, so a drug with an anti-VEGF component can slow radiographic progression without extending life, and its added toxicity can cancel the benefit; OS is the only endpoint that nets the two. FDA's oncology guidance in recent years asks sponsors to show at minimum no OS detriment, and in settings where patients have chemotherapy as an alternative it wants the survival curve to support the PFS claim. HARMONi gave a positive OS trend that missed significance, and Summit leaned on a post-hoc global analysis from September, which regulators discount because the hypothesis was written after the data. Two design traps sit underneath. First, a trial powered for PFS rarely has enough deaths to test OS, so 'trend' results are the default outcome unless OS is alpha-controlled and sized from the start. Second, when most patients come from one region, FDA asks whether the result applies to US practice, and small added cohorts cannot answer that with any power. For your own work: when a company guides to a PFS-based filing, check whether OS is a powered, alpha-protected secondary, how many deaths the interim will include, and what FDA said at the pre-BLA meeting. Summit says FDA warned it in advance, and the stock still lost 16.5% on the day.
