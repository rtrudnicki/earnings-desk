One level deeper

Why a p-value of 0.029 did not save Capricor: analysis plans, missing data and functional unblinding
HOPE-3 reported a 4.55% advantage on PUL2.0 with p=0.029, and FDA still said the trial lacked substantial evidence. The first reason is the statistical analysis plan. A p-value only controls false positives if the analysis was fixed before anyone saw outcome data; the briefing document says the plan changed after the randomized portion ended, and reviewers disputed that the trial met its endpoints under the original plan. Once that happens the agency treats the reported p-value as descriptive, whatever its size. The second reason is missing data: reviewers found the result moved depending on how dropouts were imputed, and a confidence interval whose lower bound is 0.47% has no room for that. The third is my inference, not something the letter states: hypersensitivity reactions ran about 42% on drug vs 15% on placebo, and PUL2.0 is an effort-dependent, rater-scored test, so a visible side effect can tell patients and raters who is on drug. When you size PoS on a small pivotal, ask three things before you look at the p-value: when was the plan locked, how does the estimate move under tipping-point analysis, and can a side effect break the blind on a subjective endpoint. A trial that fails any one of them deserves a haircut; HOPE-3 failed on all three, and a 9 to 3 negative panel had already said so in July.
