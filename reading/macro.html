Energy

Take: Crude is ignoring a shooting war: Brent settled flat at $91.98 Thursday with mines going off in Hormuz, then broke lower Friday morning. Surplus expectations and higher US rates are setting the price, and both benchmarks are on track for a roughly 20% drop in November.
Crude
Tankers hit mines in Hormuz and oil did not move
Iran's navy said two tankers exploded after hitting naval mines in the Strait of Hormuz Thursday (Reuters could not verify). Houthi missiles damaged Saudi Arabia's tallest tower in Riyadh. Brent settled +$0.03 at $91.98; WTI -$0.05 at $83.21, its lowest since Aug 26. The IEA puts cumulative conflict losses at 2.8B barrels of oil and 60 bcm of gas.
So what: Read: when mine strikes cannot bid crude, the risk premium is being sold and the market is trading the 2027 surplus. Friday's further drop in the snapshot fits that; one report credits US-Iran deal hopes, but I found only thin sourcing for it. Refiners and airlines benefit; high-cost shale and the Gulf fiscal breakevens take the hit. A real closure of the strait is the tail the market has stopped paying for.

Data centers

Take: Projects keep getting filed, but politics is becoming the binding constraint: Loudoun County adopted a 12-month moratorium, more counties are following, and traders cited a Democratic-led Congress slowing the build-out as a reason for Thursday's selloff. Cleanview says US construction slowed in Q3 after a record Q2.
Trend
Loudoun County, the largest US data center market, adopts a 12-month moratorium
Loudoun County, Virginia, formally adopted a 12-month moratorium on data center development. Hopkins County, Kentucky, passed a six-month moratorium on high-water-use businesses on Nov 17; Auburndale, Florida, and Montrose County, Colorado, are weighing limits. Spotsylvania County, Virginia, approved the 550-acre Crossroads campus 4-3 with more than 50 conditions.
So what: Read: new supply in Northern Virginia is frozen for a year, which raises the value of already-entitled sites and existing capacity there and pushes demand toward Texas, Indiana and Georgia. Incumbent landlords with Loudoun inventory gain pricing power; developers with unentitled Loudoun land carry a year of dead cost.

Opportunity radar

Moratoria make already-permitted and conversion sites scarce
Read, not verified: counties are freezing new data center approvals, and existing buildings already in permitting are being exempted (Auburndale's city manager said its proposed moratorium would not apply to a warehouse conversion in process; Patmos is converting a former Walmart into 50 MW outside Birmingham). The angle is to option vacant big-box or warehouse buildings with heavy existing utility service in counties that have not yet acted, file for conversion, and sell the permitted site to an operator shut out of greenfield. The operator pays.
Why now: Loudoun County adopted a 12-month moratorium this week, and Hopkins County (KY), Montrose County (CO) and Auburndale (FL) moved the same direction within days. Each freeze strands demand that needs somewhere to go.
Upside: No transaction comp found for a permitted conversion site, so size is unknown. One reference point: Giga Energy's 60 MW Texas campus leased for about $1B over 10 years. Equity selloff risk cuts the other way: if hyperscalers defer projects, the buyer pool thins.
First step: Read the Loudoun ordinance and the Auburndale draft for the exact grandfathering language, then pull utility service records for vacant big-box sites in two neighboring counties to see which already have 10 MW or more at the meter.
