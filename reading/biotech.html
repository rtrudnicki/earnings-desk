One level deeper

Why an 80-event survival win deserves a haircut: reading SELLAS's REGAL result
In a time-to-event trial the information is in the events, not the enrollment. REGAL's analysis rests on 80 deaths among 126 patients. The standard error of a log hazard ratio is about 2 divided by the square root of the event count, so 80 events gives roughly 0.22. My approximation, since the company gave no interval: an HR of 0.59 then carries a 95% confidence interval of about 0.38 to 0.91, and the reported two-sided p of 0.035 fits an upper bound close to 1. That means the data are compatible with a 9% reduction in death risk as easily as a 62% one, and a swing of five or six deaths between arms would erase significance. Three design features widen the real uncertainty beyond that interval. The control is investigator's-choice best available treatment in an open-label trial, so post-remission care, transplant referral and subsequent therapy can differ by arm without anyone intending it. A 14.7-month control median has to be checked against what CR2 AML patients not headed to transplant did historically; a control arm that underperforms inflates the HR. And a trial that ran this long accrues events slowly, which usually means the analysis timing and any interim looks consumed alpha, so ask what the prespecified final boundary was. The market's answer was a $13.00 offering against a $19.15 close: institutions bought the result at a 32% discount. When you see a small-event OS win, ask for the confidence interval, the Kaplan-Meier curves with numbers at risk, the transplant rate by arm, and the censoring pattern before you assign approval probability. FDA does approve on single trials with p near 0.035, but it reads the same four items first.
